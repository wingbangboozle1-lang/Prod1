Guide - Buying smart

Is that air fryer actually on sale? How to check the claim

By The Countertop Guide editorial desk · Listing data checked 2026-11-20 · Spec-based, not hands-on tested how we work

A struck-through number is not a saving; it is a figure whose meaning depends on which variant you are looking at, what the label says, and when it was written. We do not publish prices on this site, because a static page cannot keep one true. Here is the check you can run in ninety seconds on any listing, including the five on this site.

On this page: The picks – Full comparison – Questions readers ask

Why this site shows you no price at all

Amazon’s Associates Program Policies let a site show price and availability only when Amazon serves the data, or when it comes from the Creators or Product Advertising API; other Program Content may be stored for about a day before it must be refreshed. A page built once and published cannot meet that, so the honest option was to stop quoting figures. What we print instead is a date: the day our register read the listing. Read that as the age of the observation, not as a deal that is still there.

Ninety seconds on any listing, before you click

  1. Name the figure beside the buy button, not the one nearest it. On a page with carousels, a cheaper card two rows down is a different product. On 2026-11-20 our own register re-read caught exactly that: a low price in a widget beside one of these listings belonged to another ASIN entirely.
  2. Check what the unit is. Some buy boxes sell more than one of the item. The Chefman 6QT listing here is a two-count pack: its item details say Unit Count 2, and the price shown beside the buy button is for the pair, with a per-count figure beside it. A two-for headline next to three single-unit listings is not a bargain, it is a different quantity. The per-count figure is the one that compares, and ours are recorded with their unit counts on the register.
  3. Read what the struck-through figure is called. “List Price”, “Was”, “Typical” and a plain price are different statements with different look-back windows, and the label is the whole meaning of the discount. Amazon prints its own explanation under the List Price line on these pages; read it rather than assuming the higher number was ever the street price.
  4. Confirm the variant you are priced on. Colour, capacity and probe count are separate products to the checkout. The CHEF iQ Sense we list (B0DZFD6R9Z) is offered in one, two, three and four-probe configurations, re-read and priced separately on 2026-11-20; a saving on the smallest is not a saving on the one in your basket. The variant you have selected is stated on the page beside the buy button, because the listing, not this site, decides what a price refers to.
  5. Discount badges live where they are attached. On the same day, deal chips appeared on items inside sponsored carousels on a listing we read, not on the item the page was about. A badge next to a product is not a badge on it.
  6. Check the returns panel that Amazon shows you, now. The pages we fetched on 2026-11-20 displayed free-returns blocks with at least one no-shipping-cost option on several listings, plus holiday-season return dates. Returns are account- and date-specific, so read your own screen at the moment of purchase rather than a screenshot from anyone else, including us.

Then compare like for like: quarts per centimetre of counter depth is a number the listings actually support, and it is the comparison that decides whether you use the machine. The counter-space guide shows the arithmetic.

What a buying site can honestly tell you

Fields, capacities, dimensions, features, warranty statements and the places where a listing contradicts itself. That is our whole editorial policy, and it is why the button on every card here reads “See today’s price on Amazon” instead of showing a figure we would have to defend at midnight. Three honest examples from our own tables: the Chefman 8QT listing states 1000 W in its bullets and 1700 W in its specification table, and we publish both rather than the convenient one; the Emeril’s warranty term is not stated in its listing text at all, only a document link, so our register says so instead of guessing; and the CHEF iQ’s own details say a three-year warranty where its bullets say two years, so both are published (FLG-083).

And what to ignore

Anything that manufactures urgency. A countdown, a “few left” line, a stock of screenshots of other buyers: none of it is evidence about the machine. If a page needs one of those to make the purchase feel reasonable, that is the signal to leave, not to discount harder.

Questions readers ask

Does The Countertop Guide show Amazon prices?

No. Price and availability may only be displayed where Amazon serves the data or it comes through an API with a refresh; a static build cannot do either, so each card links to today's price and the register keeps a dated observation of what the listing showed, with unit counts and per-count figures where the listing sells a pack.

How do I know if an Amazon discount is real?

Check four things on the product page itself: which variant the buy box is priced on, what the struck-through figure is labelled, whether the offer is one unit or a pack, and whether a deal badge is attached to the item or only to a nearby carousel item. Nothing on a third-party review page can settle it.

Why does the site say 'as listed' after every spec?

Because it is a dated reading of one seller's page, not a lab result. Specs that a listing disagrees with itself on are published in both versions and flagged.
